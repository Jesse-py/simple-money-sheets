Snowball vs avalanche: which should you use?

Both methods pay the minimum on every debt and put all the extra money toward one target debt. When that debt is gone, its payment rolls over to the next target. The only difference is the order.

  • Debt snowball: target the smallest balance first. You clear whole debts sooner, and many people find those early wins keep them motivated.
  • Debt avalanche: target the highest interest rate first. This always costs the same or less in total interest, and it's usually a little faster.

When your highest-rate debt is also your smallest, the two methods are identical. When they differ, the calculator above shows exactly how much the avalanche saves, which is often less than people expect. If the gap is small, choose the method you're more likely to stick with.

Worked example: snowball vs avalanche on four debts

These are the calculator's starting numbers: a credit card ($4,200 at 24.99%, $120 minimum), a store card ($850 at 29.99%, $35 minimum), a car loan ($9,800 at 7.4%, $290 minimum), and a personal loan ($3,100 at 11.5%, $110 minimum). The minimums add up to $555, and there's $200 extra, so the budget is $755 a month.

How the debt avalanche picks the order

The avalanche ranks debts by interest rate, not by how many dollars of interest each one charges. That can look backwards at first. In the example, the car loan charges more interest each month than the store card, yet it comes last:

Interest this month is the balance times the APR divided by 12. The last column is what matters for your extra money: every $100 sent to the store card stops $2.50 of interest a month, while the same $100 on the car loan stops only $0.62. The car loan charges a lot of interest because its balance is large, not because its rate is high, so paying it early buys you the least.

When two debts have the same APR, this calculator's avalanche pays the smaller balance first, and its snowball breaks a tie in balance by paying the higher APR first. If you'd rather start with a quick win and then switch to the avalanche, pay off one small debt first, delete it from the list, and run the avalanche on the rest.

Debt avalanche spreadsheet: the formulas

If you'd rather keep the plan in your own spreadsheet, these formulas run the same month-by-month avalanche as the calculator above, in Excel or Google Sheets. They're free to copy. The layout is for four debts.

  1. In rows 2 to 5, list your debts highest APR first: name in column A, balance in B, APR in C (type it as a percent, like 29.99%), and minimum payment in D. Put your total monthly payment (all the minimums plus your extra) in F2.
  2. Starting balances go in row 7: D7 =B2, G7 =B3, J7 =B4, M7 =B5. Row 8 is month 1 (put 1 in A8).
  3. Enter the row 8 formulas below. Each debt uses three columns: owed (with interest), paid, and balance left. Column N is the leftover money.
  4. Select A8:N8 and fill down to row 400 (about 33 years), and put =A8+1 in A9 before you fill.
CellFormulaWhat it does
B8=D7*(1+$C$2/12)Debt 1 owed: last month's balance plus a month of interest
E8=G7*(1+$C$3/12)Debt 2 owed (same idea)
H8=J7*(1+$C$4/12)Debt 3 owed
K8=M7*(1+$C$5/12)Debt 4 owed
N8=$F$2-MIN($D$2,B8)-MIN($D$3,E8)-MIN($D$4,H8)-MIN($D$5,K8)Money left after every open debt gets its minimum
C8=MIN(B8,MIN($D$2,B8)+N8)Debt 1 paid: its minimum plus all the leftover, but never more than it owes
F8=MIN(E8,MIN($D$3,E8)+N8-(C8-MIN($D$2,B8)))Debt 2 paid: its minimum plus whatever leftover debt 1 didn't need
I8=MIN(H8,MIN($D$4,H8)+N8-(C8-MIN($D$2,B8))-(F8-MIN($D$3,E8)))Debt 3 paid: the same rollover, one step further

D8=B8-C8Debt 1 balance left (G8 =E8-F8, J8 =H8-I8, M8 =K8-L8 for the others)

Each debt's paid-off month is =MATCH(0,D8:D400,0) (use G, J, or M for debts 2 to 4), and the total interest is =SUM(C8:C400,F8:F400,I8:I400,L8:L400)-SUM(B2:B5). With the four debts from the worked example and 755 in F2, the sheet gives the same answer as the calculator: paid off in months 4, 18, 21, and 28, with $2,504.70 of interest.

For the snowball instead, sort the rows smallest balance first; the formulas stay the same. With three debts, delete columns K to M and the last part of the N and L formulas; for a fifth debt, add three columns and one more term the same way. How the calculation works

Each month, interest is added to each balance at its APR divided by 12. Then every open debt gets its minimum payment, and the rest of your budget goes to the target debt. If the target is paid off with money left over, the remainder goes to the next debt in line that same month. This matches how most people actually run the snowball and avalanche methods, but your lender may calculate interest daily, so real numbers can differ slightly.

Tips that make either method faster

  • Keep the total payment fixed. The rollover only works if you don't spend the freed-up minimum.
  • Ask for a lower rate. A quick call to a card issuer can sometimes cut your APR, and the avalanche benefits most from that.
  • Add windfalls to the target debt. Tax refunds, bonuses, and side income go straight to the current target.
  • Keep a small emergency buffer so a surprise expense doesn't go back on a card.

FAQ

Does this include mortgages?

You can add one, but most people leave the mortgage out of a snowball or avalanche plan because it's a large, low-rate, long-term debt. Focus on credit cards, personal loans, car loans, and student loans first.

What if my minimum payment doesn't cover the interest?

Then that balance grows every month. The calculator warns you if a debt can't be paid off within 50 years at your current budget. Raising the extra payment, or asking the lender for a lower rate or a hardship plan, are the usual fixes.

How do I enter a 0% balance transfer or promo APR?

Enter the rate you're paying now (0%), and the avalanche will put that debt last. Check your statement for the date the promo ends: after that, the regular APR applies, and some store-card deals charge deferred interest back to the purchase date if the balance isn't paid in full by then. Before the promo ends, rerun the calculator with the new rate, and aim to clear a deferred-interest balance in time. To check whether a transfer is worth its fee in the first place, use the balance transfer calculator.

Should I take a debt consolidation loan instead?

Only if its rate and fee beat paying these debts in the best order. The debt consolidation calculator compares the two at the same monthly payment, so a loan doesn't look better just because its payment is bigger.

Is the avalanche always cheaper than the snowball?

With the same monthly budget, the avalanche never costs more in total interest, because every extra dollar goes where it stops the most interest. It can't make the debt-free date later either, since the same total is paid each month. The snowball can still be the better choice for you if early paid-off debts keep you going; the calculator shows how big the gap is for your own numbers.

Are minimum payments fixed?

Here they are. On real credit cards, the minimum often falls as the balance falls. Keeping your payment at the original minimum, as this calculator does, pays the debt off faster than paying only the falling minimum. To see the difference for a single card, try the credit card payoff calculator.

Is my data saved or shared?

No. The calculation runs entirely in your browser. Your debts are kept in your browser's local storage only so they're still there if you reload the page.
