    How the estimated tax penalty works

    The IRS expects your tax to be paid as you earn it: through withholding from a paycheck, or through four estimated tax payments. For 2026 they're due April 15, June 15, and September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027. Each payment should be a quarter of your required annual payment, which is the smaller of:

    • 90% of this year's tax, or
    • 100% of last year's tax (110% if last year's adjusted gross income was over $150,000, or $75,000 married filing separately). Last year's return must have covered 12 months.

    For each due date where you paid less than that quarter, the penalty runs on the shortfall from the due date until you pay it, or until the April 15 filing deadline, whichever comes first. It's figured separately for each due date, so you can owe a penalty for June even if you caught up in September, and even if you get a refund when you file.

    Payments always go to the oldest shortfall first. If you missed June and then pay in September, that payment covers June before it counts for September.

    No penalty if any of these apply

    • This year's total tax minus your withholding is under $1,000.
    • You had no tax liability last year, you were a U.S. citizen or resident all year, and last year's return covered 12 months.
    • Your withholding plus on-time payments met the required payment for each due date.
    • For the January installment only: you file your return and pay everything you owe by the end of January (for 2026: by February 1, 2027, because January 31 is a Sunday).

    2026 penalty rates

    The penalty rate is the IRS underpayment interest rate (the federal short-term rate plus 3 points), which the IRS resets every quarter. For tax year 2026 the rate periods on Form 2210 are:

    Rate periodRateSource
    April 16 – June 30, 20266%Rev. Rul. 2026-5
    July 1 – September 30, 20267%Rev. Rul. 2026-10
    October 1 – December 31, 20267%Rev. Rul. 2026-15
    January 1 – April 15, 2027not announced yetThe IRS usually announces it in late November. The calculator assumes 7%; change it above.

    Each piece is figured as amount unpaid × days unpaid ÷ 365 × rate, split across the rate periods. At 7%, every $1,000 that stays unpaid costs about 19 cents a day, or $70 a year.

    Example: a missed September payment

    Say your 2026 tax will be $20,000 and your 2025 tax was $16,000. The required annual payment is the smaller of $18,000 (90% of $20,000) and $16,000 (100% of $16,000), so $16,000, or $4,000 per due date. You paid $4,000 in April and June, then nothing.

    • September's $4,000 runs from September 15, 2026 to April 15, 2027: 212 days. 4,000 × 212 ÷ 365 × 7% = $162.63.
    • January's $4,000 runs 90 days: 4,000 × 90 ÷ 365 × 7% = $69.04.
    • Total penalty: $231.67 if you wait until you file.

    Pay the missed $4,000 on November 15, 2026 and make the January payment on time, and the penalty drops to $46.79 (61 days on September's payment). Catching up early is the cheapest fix.

    Ways to lower or avoid the penalty

    • Pay the shortfall now. The penalty stops on the day you pay. Use IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account and choose "estimated tax" for 2026.
    • Raise your paycheck withholding if you or your spouse has a job. Withholding counts as paid in four equal parts on the due dates, no matter when in the year it was actually withheld, so extra withholding in November and December covers earlier quarters too (submit a new Form W-4 to your employer).
    • File and pay in full by February 1, 2027 to wipe out the penalty for the January installment.
    • Uneven income? If most of your income came late in the year, the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) can lower the required payments for the early quarters. This calculator doesn't do that method; it can only lower the penalty, never raise it.
    • Ask for a waiver if the shortfall came from a casualty, disaster, or other unusual circumstance, or if you retired after age 62 or became disabled in this year or last year and had reasonable cause. You request it on Form 2210.

    What this calculator doesn't cover

    • The annualized income installment method (Schedule AI), and treating withholding as paid on the actual dates (box D).
    • Farmers and fishers, who use 66⅔% instead of 90%.
    • The failure-to-pay penalty and interest on tax still unpaid after April 15. Those are separate charges.
    • State estimated tax penalties. Most states have their own rules and rates.

    FAQ

    Do I have to calculate the penalty myself?

    Usually not. If none of the Form 2210 boxes that require it apply to you, you can leave the penalty line blank and the IRS will figure it and send a bill. If you file by April 15 and pay the bill by its due date, no interest is charged on the penalty. This calculator tells you what to expect, and what paying now saves.

    I'll get a refund when I file. Can I still owe a penalty?

    Yes. The penalty is figured for each due date, so being short in June can cost a penalty even if your withholding later in the year means you're owed a refund.

    My payment was a day late. Does that matter?

    Barely. The penalty is figured by the day, so one day late on $4,000 at 7% is about 77 cents. If a due date falls on a weekend or holiday, paying on the next business day counts as on time.

    Does this calculator store my data?

    No. Everything is calculated in your browser, and nothing you type is sent anywhere.
