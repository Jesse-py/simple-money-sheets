Self-Employment Tax Calculator

Self-employment tax is the Social Security and Medicare tax you pay on freelance, 1099, and side-business profit. Enter your profit to see what you'll owe for 2026 or 2027, the half you get to deduct, and how a regular job changes the math. It follows the IRS Schedule SE and runs entirely in your browser.

How self-employment tax works

Employees split Social Security and Medicare tax with their employer: 7.65% comes out of the paycheck and the employer pays another 7.65%. When you work for yourself, you pay both halves. The IRS calculates it on Schedule SE in four steps:

  1. Take 92.35% of your net profit. This mirrors the fact that employees don't pay this tax on the employer's half. If the result is under $400, you owe no self-employment tax.
  2. Social Security: 12.4% of that amount, but only up to the year's wage base: $184,500 for 2026 and $192,300 for 2027.
  3. Medicare: 2.9% of the whole amount, with no cap.
  4. Deduct half of the total on Schedule 1 when you figure your income tax. The deduction lowers your income tax, not the self-employment tax itself.

For example, with $50,000 of profit in 2026: 92.35% is $46,175. Social Security is 12.4% of that, $5,726, and Medicare is 2.9%, $1,339. Self-employment tax is $7,065, about 14.1% of profit, and you deduct half of it, $3,532.

If you also have a W-2 job

Your W-2 wages count toward the Social Security wage base first. Once wages plus self-employment earnings pass the base, the 12.4% stops; the 2.9% Medicare part never does. So if you earned $170,000 in wages in 2026, only the first $14,500 of your self-employment earnings would get the 12.4% Social Security tax. If your wages are already over the base, you pay just the 2.9% Medicare part on your business profit.

What this calculator doesn't cover

  • Income tax. Use the quarterly estimated tax calculator for the full federal picture, including the standard deduction and the 20% qualified business income deduction.
  • The optional methods (farm and nonfarm), which let people with small or negative profits pay in a little to earn Social Security credits.
  • Church employee income, clergy, and people with an approved exemption (Form 4361 or 4029).
  • Tips and wages not on a W-2 that are subject to Social Security (Forms 4137 and 8919), which also use up the wage base.
  • State and local taxes. Most states tax your profit as income, and a few cities and transit districts have their own self-employment taxes. Check your state's rules.

FAQ

Do I have to pay self-employment tax on a small side gig?

Only if your net earnings from self-employment (92.35% of profit) are $400 or more, which means about $433 of profit. Below that you owe none, although you still report the income for income tax.

Is self-employment tax 15.3% of everything I earn?

No. It's 15.3% of 92.35% of your profit (income minus business expenses), which works out to about 14.1% of profit until you reach the Social Security wage base. Business expenses you track and deduct lower it directly.

When do I pay it?

Self-employment tax is part of the total tax on your Form 1040, so you pay it through quarterly estimated tax payments during the year, then settle up when you file. See the estimated tax calculator for the amounts and due dates.

Does a business loss give me a self-employment tax refund?

No. A loss means no self-employment tax for that business, but it doesn't create a credit against it. If you have more than one business, profits and losses are combined first.

Does this calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser, and nothing you type is sent anywhere.
