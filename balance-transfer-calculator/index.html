How a balance transfer works

A new card pays off your old card, and you owe the new card instead. The new card charges a fee for this, usually a percentage of the amount moved, and that fee is added to your new balance on day one. In return, you pay a low intro APR (often 0%) for a set number of months. When the intro period ends, whatever is left starts building interest at the card's regular APR.

So a transfer saves money when the interest you avoid during the promo is bigger than the fee, and it saves the most when you clear the balance before the promo ends.

Example: $6,000 at 22.99%

You owe $6,000 on a card at 22.99% APR and can pay $300 a month. A new card offers 0% for 18 months with a 3% transfer fee, then 24.99%.

When a transfer doesn't pay

  • You'd pay it off soon anyway. Paying $3,000 a month, that same $6,000 is gone in 3 months and costs $177.97 in interest at 22.99%, less than the $180 fee. The shorter your payoff, the less interest there is to save.
  • The promo is short and the rate after it is high. Try a 6-month intro period above: with the example's $300 payment, most of the balance is still there when the higher rate starts.
  • You keep spending on either card. New purchases on the transfer card usually don't get the intro rate. And on the old card, a fresh balance undoes the progress.
  • You miss payments. A payment more than 60 days late lets the issuer raise your APR (see the rules below), which can wipe out the savings.

The federal rules that apply

Credit card promotions in the US are governed by Regulation Z (the Truth in Lending rules, including changes from the Credit CARD Act of 2009):

  • An intro rate must last at least 6 months, and the issuer must tell you in writing, before it starts, how long it lasts and what rate applies after it (12 CFR 1026.55(b)(1)).
  • The rate can go up if a minimum payment is more than 60 days late. If that happens, the issuer must lower it again after you make six on-time minimum payments in a row (12 CFR 1026.55(b)(4)).
  • Payments above the minimum go to the highest-APR balance first (12 CFR 1026.53(a)). If you also carry purchases at a higher rate on the transfer card, your extra payments pay those first, but the minimum payment itself can be applied to the low-rate balance. That's one reason to keep the transfer card for the transfer only.

FAQ

Is the transfer fee added to my balance?

Usually, yes: it's charged to the new card when the transfer posts, so you pay it off along with the balance. This calculator adds it to the new card's starting balance. If the fee is a flat amount, enter 0% and put the amount in "Minimum fee".

What if the promo isn't 0%?

Enter the intro rate (for example 3.99%). The calculator charges that rate during the intro months and the regular APR after.

What if I can only transfer part of my balance?

Card issuers set a transfer limit, often tied to your credit line. Enter the amount you can move. The rest stays on the old card, so run the credit card payoff calculator for that part, or the debt payoff calculator to plan both cards together.

Is this the same as a deferred-interest promotion?

No. With deferred interest (common on store cards and financing deals), interest is added up from the start and charged back if any balance remains when the promo ends. This calculator assumes a true intro APR, where only the balance left after the promo pays the regular rate. If your offer's terms say "deferred interest" or "no interest if paid in full," plan to clear the full amount before the deadline.

Does a balance transfer hurt my credit?

Applying for a new card usually means a hard inquiry, and a new account lowers the average age of your accounts. Paying the balance down lowers how much of your credit you're using. Credit scoring models weigh these differently, so we can't give you a number.

Does this calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser.
