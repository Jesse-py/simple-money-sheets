Freelancer Tax Tracker spreadsheet

Nobody withholds tax from freelance income, so you pay it yourself, four times a year. Log what you earn and spend, and this spreadsheet sorts your expenses into Schedule C lines, works out each quarterly payment with the IRS worksheet, and shows what you've paid against every due date.

What's inside

  • Income log: 1,000 rows for every payment you receive, with a 1099 flag so you can compare against the 1099-NEC and 1099-K forms you get in January.
  • Expense log: 2,000 rows. Pick the Schedule C line from a dropdown that lists every expense line on the 2026 Schedule C, plus cost of goods sold and the home office. Enter business meals at full cost; the sheet counts the deductible 50%.
  • Summary: income and expenses in Schedule C order, by estimated-tax period and for the year, down to net profit (line 31). Handy at tax time, whether you file yourself or hand it to a preparer.
  • Estimate: the Form 1040-ES Estimated Tax Worksheet and Self-Employment Tax Worksheet, line by line. It covers self-employment tax and the Social Security wage base, the half-SE-tax deduction, the standard or itemized deduction, the QBI deduction, the brackets, and the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax. Then it applies the safe harbor rules and the $1,000 rule to give your quarterly payment.
  • Set aside per $1: the share of each payment you receive to move into savings for federal tax, worked out from your own numbers.
  • Payments: the four due dates, what's required by each, what you've paid, and a status that turns red if you fall behind.
  • Tax years 2026 and 2027 for all four filing statuses, including W-2 wages from a job, a spouse's wages, and withholding. The numbers are on a Tax Tables tab with their IRS and SSA sources.

The estimate uses the same math as our free quarterly estimated tax calculator. Before release, we checked the spreadsheet against it on hundreds of tax situations (both years, every filing status, W-2 wages, losses, high incomes), and every formula was recalculated in Excel with no differences.

Estimated tax periods aren't calendar quarters

The IRS splits the year into four uneven periods, and the payment for each one is due a couple of weeks after it ends. The tracker totals your income and expenses by these periods, so you can see what each payment covers.

PaymentIncome earnedDue (2026 tax year)Due (2027 tax year)
1Jan 1 – Mar 31Apr 15, 2026Apr 15, 2027
2Apr 1 – May 31Jun 15, 2026Jun 15, 2027
3Jun 1 – Aug 31Sep 15, 2026Sep 15, 2027
4Sep 1 – Dec 31Jan 15, 2027Jan 18, 2028*

2026 dates from the 2026 Form 1040-ES. *Jan 15, 2028 is a Saturday and Jan 17 is Martin Luther King Jr. Day, so the payment moves to the next business day; we'll confirm it when the IRS publishes the 2027 Form 1040-ES.

You avoid the underpayment penalty if what you pay by each date adds up to at least a quarter of the required annual payment: the smaller of 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000). Using last year's tax is the simplest safe harbor, because it doesn't change when your income does. The tracker shows both and uses the smaller.

FAQ

Who is it for?

Freelancers, contractors, gig workers, and side businesses that file Schedule C, including people who also have a W-2 job. It's for federal tax; set aside state tax separately.

What doesn't it cover?

State and local tax, capital gains and qualified dividend rates, the Net Investment Income Tax, AMT, the annualized income installment method, and farmer/fisherman rules. The QBI deduction assumes you have no employees and no business property, which is true for most freelancers. If those apply to you, treat the numbers as a starting point and check with a tax professional.

What if my income changes during the year?

Update your expected profit on the Setup tab (or leave it blank and the tracker scales up the profit you've logged so far), and the quarterly amount updates. If you use last year's tax as your safe harbor, the required payment stays the same.

Does it work in Google Sheets?

It's made for Microsoft Excel and tested there. It uses standard functions (SUMIFS, INDEX, MATCH, and the like) that Google Sheets and LibreOffice also have, with no macros, so it should work there, but we haven't tested it in those yet.

Is this tax advice?

No. It's a record-keeping and planning tool that follows the published IRS worksheets. Your actual return may differ.
