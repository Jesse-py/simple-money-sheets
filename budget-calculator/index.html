What counts as a need, a want, or savings?

Needs (about 50%)

Bills you'd still have to pay if you cut back to the basics: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation to work, childcare, and the minimum payments on your debts. Shopping for a car? The car affordability calculator shows what payment fits next to your other needs.

Wants (about 30%)

Things that make life better but could be cut: dining out, streaming and subscriptions, travel, hobbies, the upgrade from a basic phone plan, and most shopping.

Savings and debt payoff (about 20%)

Emergency fund contributions, retirement savings beyond any payroll deduction, investing, and any debt payment above the minimum. Extra debt payments go here because they improve your net worth just like saving does. Saving for something specific? The savings goal calculator turns a goal and a date into a monthly amount, and the compound interest calculator shows how a monthly amount adds up over the years.

Example: $2,000 every two weeks

Paid every two weeks, you get 26 paychecks a year: $2,000 × 26 = $52,000 a year, or about $4,333 a month. The 50/30/20 split is:

Working from the paycheck is often easier: on payday, $400 moves to savings, and you know $1,000 of what's left is for needs. Your actual bills don't fall evenly across paychecks, so keep a small buffer in checking for the paycheck that pays rent.

50/30/20 on a weekly paycheck

Paid every week? Split each paycheck the same way: $800 a week take-home is $400 for needs, $240 for wants, and $160 for savings or extra debt payments. Over a year that's $800 × 52 = $41,600, or about $3,467 a month. Paid every two weeks? Halve your paycheck and use the same row: a $1,600 biweekly paycheck works out to the same $800 a week.

Notice the last column isn't weekly pay × 4. A year has 52 weeks, so a month averages 52 ÷ 12 ≈ 4.33 weeks. Budgeting on 4 weeks a month undercounts your income by about 8%; in practice that extra shows up as a fifth payday in about four months of the year. A simple plan: budget your monthly bills on four paychecks and send each fifth paycheck straight to savings or debt.

Bills don't arrive weekly, so on a weekly budget set aside each monthly bill's weekly share every payday: the bill × 12 ÷ 52. Rent of $1,200 a month is about $277 a week. The calculator's "Per week" column does this for any pay schedule.

Why use take-home pay?

The rule, popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in All Your Worth, uses after-tax income: what actually lands in your account. If your employer already takes 401(k) or other retirement contributions out of your paycheck, that money is savings you never see. Enter it in the "Retirement already taken out" box: the calculator adds it back to your pay and counts it toward the 20%. In the example above, $150 a paycheck into a 401(k) makes the base $2,150, so the 20% is $430 a paycheck, and $280 of it is still yours to move to savings.

When 50/30/20 doesn't fit

In expensive cities, or on a lower income, needs can easily take more than half your pay. That's why the calculator offers 60/30/10 and 70/20/10 splits, or a custom split of your own. The goal is the habit: know where every dollar goes and send something to savings every month. If you're paying off high-interest debt, many people temporarily shift money from wants to the 20% to get out faster.

FAQ

How do I convert biweekly pay to monthly?

Multiply your paycheck by 26 and divide by 12. The calculator does this for you when you choose "Every 2 weeks". In two months of the year you'll get a third paycheck, which is a great time to boost savings.

How do I use the 50/30/20 rule on a weekly paycheck?

Take each week's take-home pay and split it: half to needs, 30% to wants, 20% to savings. On $800 a week that's $400, $240, and $160. Choose "Weekly" as the pay period and the calculator shows the weekly, monthly, and yearly amounts.

Is 20/30/50 the same as 50/30/20?

Yes. It's the same three shares (20% savings, 30% wants, 50% needs) written in the other order, often to stress paying yourself first: move the 20% on payday, then live on the rest.

Is 50/30/20 based on gross or net income?

Net: your pay after taxes. Using gross pay would set targets with money you never receive. The one thing to add back is retirement saving taken out of your paycheck, because it's part of your 20% (use the "Retirement already taken out" box).

Is a car payment a need or a want?

The minimum payment counts as a need if you need the car to get to work. Paying extra on the loan counts toward savings and debt payoff.

Does this calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser.
