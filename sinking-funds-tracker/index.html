Why sinking funds work

Most budget-busting bills aren't surprises. Car insurance renews on the same date every six months, the holidays come every December, and cars always need tires eventually. A sinking fund spreads each of those costs over the months before it's due. $720 of car insurance due in six months becomes $120 a month. When the bill arrives, you pay it from savings instead of a credit card.

One goal is easy to do in your head. Ten goals with different due dates aren't, especially once you start spending from some of them. That's what the tracker is for. You can keep all the money in one savings account; the tracker shows how much of the balance belongs to each fund.

FAQ

Does it work in Google Sheets?

Yes. Upload the .xlsx file to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets. All formulas work there. It also works in Excel 2016 and later, Excel for Mac, and LibreOffice. (The small progress bars are Excel-only; the percentages show everywhere.)

How is "needed per month" worked out?

It takes what's still needed and divides it by the deposits you can still make before the month the money is due, counting this month. On March 10, a bill due July 1 leaves four deposits: March, April, May, and June.

What happens after I pay the bill?

Log the payment as a negative amount. For a cost that repeats, like an insurance premium, change the due date to the next one, and the fund starts filling up again.
