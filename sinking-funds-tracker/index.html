Why sinking funds work

Most budget-busting bills aren't surprises. Car insurance renews on the same date every six months, the holidays come every December, and cars always need tires eventually. A sinking fund spreads each of those costs over the months before it's due. $720 of car insurance due in six months becomes $120 a month. When the bill arrives, you pay it from savings instead of a credit card.

One goal is easy to do in your head. Ten goals with different due dates aren't, especially once you start spending from some of them. That's what the tracker is for. You can keep all the money in one savings account; the tracker shows how much of the balance belongs to each fund.

Common sinking funds

Anything you know is coming but don't pay every month. Use your own bills for the goals; these are the usual suspects:

  • Car: insurance if you pay every 6 or 12 months, registration and inspection, tires, repairs and maintenance (open-ended), and saving for the next car.
  • Home: property tax and homeowners insurance if they aren't in your mortgage payment, repairs and maintenance, furniture, and appliances.
  • Once a year: holidays and gifts, birthdays, back-to-school, annual subscriptions and memberships, and tax prep.
  • Health and pets: your insurance deductible, dental and glasses, and vet visits.
  • Fun: vacation, weddings you're going to, and a new phone or laptop.

FAQ

What's the difference between a sinking fund and an emergency fund?

A sinking fund is for a cost you can see coming, like a premium due in June. An emergency fund is for what you can't predict, like a job loss. Keep them apart in your head (or in the tracker) so paying an expected bill doesn't drain your safety net.

Is my list saved?

Yes, in this browser on this device only (local storage). Nothing is sent to us. Clearing your browser data or using another device starts over with the example.

Does it work in Google Sheets?

It's made for Microsoft Excel and tested there: we recalculate every formula in Excel and check the results. It uses only standard functions (like SUMIFS, INDEX, and MATCH) that have been in Excel since 2010 and that Google Sheets and LibreOffice also have, with no macros, so you can upload it to Google Drive and open it with Google Sheets. We haven't tested every feature in Google Sheets or LibreOffice yet, though, and some formatting may look different there. The small progress bars may not show outside Excel; the percentages always do.

How is "needed per month" worked out?

It takes what's still needed and divides it by the deposits you can still make before the month the money is due, counting this month. On March 10, a bill due July 1 leaves four deposits: March, April, May, and June. If the due month has arrived and the fund is still short, the whole shortfall shows as "Due now" and is counted in full. Per paycheck is the monthly total times 12, divided by your paychecks a year (26 if every 2 weeks).

What happens after I pay the bill?

Log the payment as a negative amount. For a cost that repeats, like an insurance premium, change the due date to the next one, and the fund starts filling up again.
