How the comparison works

Keep paying: you keep paying the same total each month. When one debt is paid off, its payment goes to the debt with the highest APR (the avalanche order), so the total never drops until you're debt-free. That's the cheapest way to pay off what you have now, so the loan has to beat your best plan, not a plan of only paying minimums.

Consolidate: a fixed-rate loan pays off every debt on day one, and you make one payment for the loan's term. The origination fee is part of what the loan costs you.

At the same payment: a loan with a bigger payment than yours can look like a winner just because it pays the debt off faster, and you could do that without a loan. So the calculator also compares both sides at the same monthly payment. That number is what the loan's rate and fee are actually worth.

Example: $8,500 on three cards

Card A: $4,500 at 24.99%, paying $135. Card B: $2,800 at 21.49%, paying $85. A store card: $1,200 at 29.99%, paying $50. That's $270 a month. The loan offer: 13.99% for 36 months with a 5% origination fee taken out of the loan.

To have $8,500 left after a 5% fee, you borrow $8,947.37. The 36-month loan saves $2,710.54 and ends 15 months sooner, but its payment is $35.76 a month higher than you pay now. Paying that same $305.76 a month on the cards themselves would take 42 months and cost $4,046.76 in interest, so the loan's lower rate is worth $1,539.53; the rest of the savings comes from paying more each month. The 60-month loan has the lower payment and still saves $1,229.17, but you'd be paying for 9 months longer. Pay your current $270 into the 60-month loan instead, and it's gone in 43 months for $2,874.98, if the loan has no prepayment penalty. The fee lifts the 36-month loan's APR from 13.99% to 17.64%.

When consolidation doesn't pay

  • The fee eats the savings. A 5% to 8% fee on a small or short payoff can cost more than the interest you'd avoid. Try a 0% fee above to see how much of the gap is the fee.
  • The longer term hides the cost. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warns that a lower monthly payment "may be because you're paying over a longer time." Look at total cost, not just the payment.
  • Your rate isn't much lower. If your debts are already near the loan's rate, the fee usually makes the loan a loss.
  • The cards fill up again. Paying off cards frees up their limits. New balances on top of the loan leave you with more debt than you started with.
  • The loan is secured by your home. A home equity loan can have a lower rate, but as the CFPB puts it, "If you don't pay back the loan, you could lose your home in foreclosure."

Interest rate vs APR on a consolidation loan

Under the Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z), loan fees are part of the finance charge (12 CFR 1026.4(b)(3)). The "amount financed" on your disclosure is the loan amount minus any prepaid finance charge such as an origination fee taken out up front (12 CFR 1026.18(b)), and the APR is the yearly cost of credit on that amount (1026.18(e)). So a loan with a fee always has an APR above its interest rate. When you compare offers, compare APRs with each other, or use this calculator with each offer's interest rate and fee.

FAQ

Should I enter minimum payments or what I actually pay?

What you actually pay. If you pay more than the minimums now, the "keep paying" plan finishes sooner, and the loan has to beat that.

Is a balance transfer card better?

It can be, for card debt you can clear within the promo. The balance transfer calculator compares the transfer fee with the interest saved and shows what's left when the promo ends.

What's the difference between "taken out" and "added"?

Most personal loans take the fee out of the money you receive, so to pay off $8,500 at 5% you need to borrow $8,947.37. If your lender adds the fee to the balance instead, you'd borrow $8,925.00. The difference is small; use whichever your offer describes.

Does the calculator include a prepayment penalty?

No. Many personal loans have none, but check your offer. The "pay your current amount into the loan" figure assumes extra payments are allowed with no fee.

Does this calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser.
