How credit card interest works

Your APR is a yearly rate, but interest is charged every billing cycle. Most issuers divide the APR by 365 to get a daily rate and apply it to your average daily balance. This calculator uses the simpler version: APR ÷ 12 for each monthly payment (APR ÷ 26 for biweekly, ÷ 52 for weekly), so your statements may differ by a few dollars. At 22.99% APR, a $5,000 balance builds up about $96 of interest in a month. Every payment covers that interest first, and only the rest reduces what you owe.

Why minimum payments take so long

A minimum payment is designed to keep your account in good standing, not to get you out of debt. With a "1% plus interest" minimum, only about 1% of your balance goes to principal each month. As the balance shrinks, so does the minimum, which stretches the payoff out for years. Use the comparison above to see how many years and how much interest a fixed payment saves you.

Your statement shows this too. Under the Credit CARD Act of 2009, US card statements include a "Minimum Payment Warning" box with how long minimum payments would take and what they would cost in total, plus the monthly payment that would clear the balance in three years.

Does paying every two weeks help?

A little, and mostly for one reason: there are 26 two-week periods in a year, so paying half your monthly amount every two weeks adds up to 13 monthly payments instead of 12. Paying earlier also lowers your average daily balance, which is what the interest is charged on, but that part is small.

Take a $5,000 balance at 22.99% APR. Paying $200 once a month clears it in 35 months with about $1,871 of interest. Paying $100 every two weeks clears it in 67 payments (about 31 months) with about $1,629 of interest: $242 less and 4 months sooner. If you instead pay $92.31 every two weeks, the same $2,400 a year as $200 a month, interest is about $1,836. So the earlier timing saves only about $35; the extra $200 a year does the rest.

Your card still has one due date a month, and the minimum has to be paid by then. Every month has at least two biweekly payments, so two half payments that add up to the minimum keep you on time. Check that your issuer applies extra payments to the balance right away; most do.

Ways to pay off a card faster

  • Pay a fixed amount, not the minimum. Keep paying your first month's amount even as the minimum drops. That alone usually cuts years off.
  • Find the money in your budget. The 50/30/20 budget calculator shows how much of your pay could go to savings and extra debt payments.
  • Ask for a lower rate. If you've paid on time, calling your issuer to ask for a lower APR costs nothing, and it sometimes works.
  • Consider a balance transfer carefully. A 0% intro APR can save a lot, but transfer fees are often 3% to 5% of the balance, and the rate jumps when the promotion ends. The balance transfer calculator compares keeping your card with transferring, fee included.
  • Several cards? Put extra money on one card at a time: the highest APR first (avalanche) or the smallest balance first (snowball). The snowball vs avalanche calculator compares both.

FAQ

Does this include new purchases?

No. It assumes you stop using the card while you pay it off. New charges add to the balance and push your payoff date back.

Can I see a credit card amortization table?

Yes. The schedule under your plan lists every payment with the interest, the principal, and the balance left after it. Switch "I pay" to every two weeks or every week to see one row per payment.

What if my payment doesn't cover the interest?

Then the balance never goes down. The calculator warns you and shows the smallest payment that would make progress.

Can I use this for a car loan or personal loan?

Installment loans have a fixed payment and term, so use the loan calculator with extra payments instead. It shows the amortization schedule and how much paying extra saves.

Does this calculator store my data?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser.
